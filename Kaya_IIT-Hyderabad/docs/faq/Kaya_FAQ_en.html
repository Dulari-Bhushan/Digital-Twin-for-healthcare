1What is Kaya?

A digital twin of each patient's glucose, kidneys, eyes and liver. The doctor gets one live priority list, alerts with the reason behind them, the patient's records, and can message the patient, send a prescription and try a medicine change on the twin first. The patient gets a simple phone app.

2Who uses it, and who pays?

The doctor and clinic use it every day. In Japan, insurers and private clinics fund an annual subscription per enrolled patient, because they save most when dialysis is avoided; in India, hospital and lab chains pay. No price is set yet. We will agree it with pilot partners.

3Why kidneys and eyes?

Diabetes is the top cause of new dialysis [1], yet the yearly tests that catch damage early are often skipped [3]. Kaya links glucose, kidney and eye risk with whether those tests happened, and helps the doctor act.

4How does it help when clinics close early and on weekends?

Patients can ask for a slot in a calendar at any time and send reports from home. The doctor reviews them remotely, sends advice or a prescription, and asks patients to come in only when it matters. Reception confirms requests, keeps urgent slots, and tells patients automatically if the doctor is called away.

5Does it replace the doctor?

No. It is decision support. It does not diagnose or advise on doses; the doctor decides and signs. The patient assistant refuses medicine and dose questions and tells anyone with emergency symptoms to call 119.

6How accurate is it?

On 34 real glucose-sensor recordings, the 2-hour forecast error is 28 mg/dL, against 37 for assuming no change. It catches 72% of low-sugar episodes, a median 45 minutes ahead, at about one false alert per patient-day. It also spots liver fibrosis risk better than the standard FIB-4 score (0.83 vs 0.63). Results are from held-out data and not yet tested in Japan. [8]

7Is patient data safe?

Yes. The prototype holds synthetic data only. Kaya is designed around Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI), the government guidelines for the safe management of medical information systems, and the HL7 FHIR standard: patient consent, encrypted storage and transfer, role-based access with an audit log, and no patient names or IDs shared with outside services. A real deployment goes through a full security review first.

8How does it fit Japan's care system?

Patients usually start at a clinic or pharmacy, and a first visit to a large hospital without a clinic's letter costs at least ¥7,000 extra [7], so the clinic doctor is the right user. Kaya prepares a referral letter (紹介状) in Japanese for the doctor to sign.

9What does a clinic need to start?

A web browser for the doctor and reception, and a phone for each patient. No new devices or sensors are required. The patient app speaks Japanese and English, with Hindi, Telugu and Kannada also available.

10How is it different from other diabetes apps?

The apps we found log glucose or coach lifestyle. We did not find one that links glucose, kidney and eye risk and whether the yearly tests happened, in one workspace for the doctor with booking, messaging, prescribing and referral. We looked at public information only; this is to be verified.
